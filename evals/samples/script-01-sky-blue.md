# script-01-sky-blue

## Prompt

```txt
Write a 2-minute YouTube script explaining why the sky is blue.
```

## What to check

Opening states the question or misconception; sections hand off; one-breath sentences; notes in brackets on their own lines.

## Default output

### Why Is the Sky Blue? (2-Minute YouTube Script)

**Estimated runtime:** ~2 minutes (about 300 words of narration)

---

**[0:00 – 0:10] HOOK**

*[Visual: Time-lapse of a bright blue sky with clouds drifting by.]*

**NARRATOR:**
Look up on a clear day and you'll see it: blue. Everywhere. But here's the strange part: sunlight isn't blue. So why is the sky?

---

**[0:10 – 0:35] WHAT SUNLIGHT IS MADE OF**

*[Visual: A beam of white light passing through a prism, splitting into a rainbow.]*

**NARRATOR:**
Sunlight looks white, but it's actually a mix of every color of the rainbow: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, and violet. Each color travels as a wave, and each one has a different wavelength. Red light has long, lazy waves. Blue and violet have short, tight ones.

*[On-screen graphic: Wavelengths labeled, red ~700 nm, blue ~450 nm.]*

---

**[0:35 – 1:05] RAYLEIGH SCATTERING**

*[Visual: Animation of sunlight entering Earth's atmosphere and hitting tiny gas molecules.]*

**NARRATOR:**
When sunlight enters our atmosphere, it runs into tiny molecules of nitrogen and oxygen. These molecules are much smaller than the wavelengths of light, and they scatter light in all directions.

But they don't scatter every color equally. Shorter wavelengths get scattered much more strongly. Blue light is scattered about five times more than red light.

*[On-screen text: "Rayleigh Scattering"]*

This effect is called **Rayleigh scattering**, named after the British physicist Lord Rayleigh. So as sunlight passes through the air, blue light gets bounced all over the sky, and that scattered blue light reaches your eyes from every direction.

---

**[1:05 – 1:30] WHY NOT VIOLET?**

*[Visual: Split screen with a violet sky on one side and a blue sky on the other, with a question mark.]*

**NARRATOR:**
Wait. If shorter wavelengths scatter more, and violet is even shorter than blue, why isn't the sky purple?

Three reasons. First, the sun gives off less violet light than blue. Second, some violet light is absorbed high in the atmosphere. And third, our eyes are simply more sensitive to blue. Put it all together, and our brains see the sky as blue.

---

**[1:30 – 1:50] BONUS: SUNSETS**

*[Visual: Gorgeous time-lapse of an orange and red sunset.]*

**NARRATOR:**
And that also explains sunsets. When the sun is low on the horizon, its light has to travel through much more atmosphere to reach you. Most of the blue gets scattered away along the way, leaving the warm reds and oranges to paint the sky.

---

**[1:50 – 2:00] OUTRO**

*[Visual: Narrator on camera, or a shot looking up at the sky.]*

**NARRATOR:**
So the next time you look up, remember: you're seeing sunlight bouncing off trillions of tiny molecules. If you learned something new, hit like, subscribe, and let me know in the comments what everyday mystery we should explain next!

*[End screen: Subscribe button and suggested videos.]*

---

**Production notes:**
- Keep the pacing brisk and conversational, about 150 words per minute.
- A simple animated diagram for the scattering section will help viewers a lot.
- Optional: Add a quick on-screen "Fun Fact" pop-up mentioning that on Mars, the daytime sky looks butterscotch and the sunsets look blue.

## YAH output

[VISUAL: bright blue sky over sand dunes, no water anywhere in frame]
Ask why the sky is blue, and a lot of people will tell you it's reflecting the ocean. So how is the sky over the Sahara just as blue, hundreds of miles from any sea?

The answer starts with sunlight.

[VISUAL: white light entering a prism and fanning out into a rainbow]
Sunlight looks white because it's every color mixed together. Shine it through a prism and the colors spread out, red at one end and violet at the other. Each color is light with a different wavelength. Red has the longest waves. Blue and violet have the shortest.

[VISUAL: sunlight entering the atmosphere, tiny dots for air molecules]
On its way down to you, that light passes through miles of air. Air is mostly nitrogen and oxygen molecules, and they're far smaller than a wave of light. When light hits one of them, it gets sent off in a new direction. That's called scattering.

[ON-SCREEN TEXT: Rayleigh scattering]
Short waves scatter much more than long ones. Blue light scatters about five times more than red. Physicists call this Rayleigh scattering, after Lord Rayleigh, who worked out the math in the 1870s.

[VISUAL: blue arrows bouncing across the sky toward a person on the ground]
So blue light gets bounced all over the sky. Look at any part of the sky away from the sun. Some of that scattered blue is heading toward your eyes from there.

[BEAT: pause]
Violet waves are even shorter than blue ones, though. So why isn't the sky violet?

Sunlight has less violet in it than blue to begin with. On top of that, your eyes are much more sensitive to blue than to violet. Your brain takes the mix of scattered light that reaches you and reads it as pale blue.

[VISUAL: sunset, sun low on the horizon]
The same scattering explains sunsets. When the sun is low, its light has to cross far more air to reach you. Most of the blue gets scattered away before it arrives, and what's left is orange and red.

[VISUAL: a lake under blue sky, then the same lake under heavy cloud]
As for the ocean, it works the other way around. Calm water reflects the sky above it, which is why a lake looks gray on a cloudy day. Water also absorbs a little red light, so deep water has a faint blue of its own.
