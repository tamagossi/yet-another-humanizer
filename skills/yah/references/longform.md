# Long-form mode

For articles, blog posts, essays and newsletters, and for edits of existing text.

## Drafting a new piece

**State the position in the opening.** The first paragraph says what the piece claims or recommends. The rest supports it, tests it, or admits where it's weak. A piece that surveys "both sides" and concludes nothing reads as generated.

**Build each section around one claim.** Give the claim, then the evidence or example that makes it believable. Cut sections that only restate earlier ones.

**Mark missing experience; never invent it.** First-hand experience is what readers and search engines value most, and it's the one thing you can't supply. When the piece needs it, insert a placeholder the writer can fill:

> [YOUR EXPERIENCE: a time you shipped without tests and what broke, with the date or project name]

Describe the experience needed specifically enough that the writer knows what to write. Don't write a fake anecdote, even as an example.

**Keep the writer's edge.** Strong opinions, blunt words, humor and admitted doubt belong to the writer. Don't sand them into a safer tone.

**End on the last concrete point.** No "In conclusion", no summary of what the reader just read, no "the future looks bright". If the piece needs closure, end on a takeaway or the next action.

## People-first search check

Run this when the user asks for SEO or says the piece is for search. It follows Google Search Central's guidance on helpful, people-first content.

Check the draft for:

1. **Original information:** does it say something a reader can't get from the top results?
2. **First-hand experience:** does it show the writer actually did or used the thing? (Placeholders count as gaps.)
3. **Value beyond rewording:** would it still be worth reading if the reader had seen the sources it draws on?
4. **An honest title:** does the title describe the piece without exaggerating or baiting?

Report each gap after the draft, with what would close it: "No first-hand data. Add your own load-test numbers from the migration." Never add repeated keywords to the text, and don't promise rankings.

## Editing existing text

Editing has one hard rule: the edit says what the original said, no more and no less.

1. **List the claims** in the original before changing anything: every fact, name, number, date, quote, and stated opinion.
2. **Edit for the reader.** Fix tells, tangles and repetition. Leave strong sentences alone. A rough draft with a real voice should still sound like the same person.
3. **Check the list against the edit.** Every claim appears; no new claim appears. A dropped claim is an error unless it was pure filler (a stock opener or closer, a repeated point, an item in a triad that says nothing checkable). A vague claim the tell catalog would cut ("trusted by industry leaders") stays in an edit: it's the writer's call, so keep it and list it in step 4.
4. **List what you couldn't fix.** After the edit, name each sentence that needs information you don't have, and say what would fix it.

Keep code blocks, inline code, commands, paths, URLs, data and quotations exactly as they were. When editing a file, write only the final text to it and tell the user what changed in a line or two.

If you reorganize the structure, say why in one line after the edit.
